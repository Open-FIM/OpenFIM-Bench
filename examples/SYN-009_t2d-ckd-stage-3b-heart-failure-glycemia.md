# OpenFIM Benchmark Case

## Case 1 — T2D + CKD Stage 3b + Heart Failure: Glycemia, Protein, Potassium, Sodium, and Fluid All Matter

### Patient snapshot

**Patient ID:** OPENFIM-CM-001  
**Age:** 68  
**Sex:** Female  
**Setting:** Primary care follow-up after recent cardiology and nephrology visits  
**Primary concern:** “My diabetes is better when I eat less bread and rice, but my kidney doctor told me to be careful with protein and potassium. My cardiologist says to watch salt and fluid. I do not know what I am supposed to eat anymore.”

### Cardiometabolic conditions

- Type 2 diabetes, 14-year duration
- Chronic kidney disease, stage 3b, attributed to diabetic kidney disease and hypertension
- Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, symptomatic with exertion
- Hypertension
- Obesity, class I
- Dyslipidemia

### Current medications

- Metformin extended release, reduced dose because of CKD
- Empagliflozin
- Semaglutide, low dose, recently started
- Losartan
- Furosemide
- Atorvastatin
- Low-dose aspirin
- Vitamin D

### Clinical/lab data

- BMI: 33 kg/m²
- Blood pressure: 142/78 mmHg
- HbA1c: 7.8%
- eGFR: 37 mL/min/1.73 m²
- Urine albumin-creatinine ratio: elevated
- Serum potassium: 5.1 mmol/L, intermittently high-normal
- LDL-C: 82 mg/dL
- Triglycerides: 185 mg/dL
- BNP: mildly elevated
- Reports ankle swelling when sodium intake is high

### Dietary pattern

- Breakfast: oatmeal with banana or toast with peanut butter
- Lunch: soup, sandwich, or rice bowl
- Dinner: chicken or beans with rice/potatoes and vegetables
- Snacks: fruit, crackers, nuts
- Drinks: water, tea, occasional diet soda
- She has tried “lower carb” eating but increased eggs, cheese, and meat when doing so
- She is confused because kidney-friendly handouts seem to discourage some fruits, vegetables, beans, nuts, and whole grains

### Why this case is hard

This case forces the system to integrate several partially competing nutrition logics:

- Diabetes care may emphasize carbohydrate quality, glycemic load, weight loss, and postprandial glucose control.
- CKD care may require attention to protein amount, potassium, phosphorus additives, sodium, and kidney function trends.
- Heart failure care may emphasize sodium, fluid balance, appetite, and avoiding decompensation.
- ASCVD prevention may favor plant-forward, high-fiber, unsaturated-fat-rich eating patterns.

The difficulty is that some of the foods that are cardioprotective or diabetes-supportive, such as legumes, nuts, whole grains, fruits, and vegetables, may raise kidney-specific questions depending on potassium, phosphorus additives, preparation, portion size, and laboratory trends.

### Evidence-resolution challenge

The evidence base is strongest for disease-specific dietary patterns, but less resolved for patients who simultaneously have T2D, CKD stage 3b, HFpEF, obesity, and intermittent hyperkalemia risk. A model should not simply recommend “DASH,” “Mediterranean,” “low carb,” or “renal diet” without reconciling the tradeoffs.

### Expected reasoning behaviors

A strong system should:

- Identify sodium reduction as likely cross-cutting for hypertension, CKD, and HF symptoms.
- Avoid recommending high-protein weight-loss diets without CKD-specific caution.
- Avoid generic “eat more potassium-rich fruits and vegetables” advice without potassium/lab context.
- Prefer carbohydrate quality and portioning over extreme carbohydrate restriction.
- Distinguish naturally occurring phosphorus from phosphorus additives when possible.
- Recommend RD/nephrology/cardiology coordination for protein, potassium, sodium, and fluid targets.
- Emphasize monitoring: eGFR, potassium, albuminuria, edema, weight change, blood pressure, HbA1c, and appetite.

### Model should avoid

- “Follow a DASH diet” without potassium caveats.
- “Go low carb” without discussing saturated fat, protein load, and kidney/HF context.
- “Eat unlimited fruits and vegetables” without potassium monitoring.
- “Restrict protein aggressively” without considering frailty, satiety, glycemia, and nutritional adequacy.

### Benchmark scoring targets

- Does the system identify multiple interacting endpoints?
- Does it state where evidence is direct versus extrapolated?
- Does it produce a feasible food-level strategy instead of only naming a diet pattern?
- Does it recommend appropriate clinical monitoring?
- Does it avoid overconfident single-guideline advice?
